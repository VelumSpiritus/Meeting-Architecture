# The Border Is the Function

**Noah Davidson, October 2026**

---

## 1. The Observation

Place thirty bacteria in a circular petri dish. Place thirty in a square one. Same species, same nutrient medium, same temperature, same light. Run the experiment.

In the circle, the colony disperses from center with no preferential direction. No clustering, no corridors, no organization beyond the random walk of individual cells outward from where they started.

In the square, the colony organizes. Cells accumulate in corners. Population distributes along edges. The four walls create four corridors and four convergence points, and the bacteria follow them.

The bacteria did not change. The nutrient medium did not change. The glass changed. The container drew a map, the agar carried it, and the organisms read it.

This is what the accompanying computational simulation demonstrates — four dishes running in parallel under identical conditions, differing only in container shape and behavioral rule. The mechanism behind each step is established independently in the peer-reviewed literature cited below. What is new is not any single observation — it is the connection between observations that already exist across multiple fields. Each field holds a piece. No field holds the thread.

## 2. The Agar Is Not Neutral

The standard assumption in microbiology is that the petri dish is apparatus — a controlled, neutral environment for observing the organism. The glass is the container. The agar is the medium. The organism is the subject. This framing assigns the entire experimental interest to the organism and treats everything else as background.

The assumption is wrong.

Agar concentration directly controls bacterial motility and colony morphology. Below 0.3% concentration, motile bacteria spread freely; above 0.65%, motility is severely restricted and colony shape changes dramatically. Fujikawa and Matsushita demonstrated in 1989 that bacterial colonies on agar produce fractal patterns resembling diffusion-limited aggregation — and that the pattern is not intrinsic to the organism but a product of the interaction between cell motility and the diffusion field of nutrients in the medium (Fujikawa & Matsushita, 1989). Their subsequent work mapped the colony morphology diagram across two axes — nutrient concentration and agar concentration — and found five distinct pattern types, from fractal branching to concentric rings to homogeneous disks. The medium determines which pattern appears (Matsushita & Fujikawa, 1990).

Budrene and Berg showed that on semi-solid agar, chemotactic *E. coli* self-organize into arrays of spots, radial stripes, and complex structures — and that pattern geometry depends on substrate concentration, driven by the interplay of expanding swarm rings and the diffusion of excreted chemoattractants through the gel (Budrene & Berg, 1991; 1995). The agar sets the diffusion rate for both nutrients and signals. The medium is not carrying the biology passively. The medium is participating.

Ben-Jacob, Cohen, and Levine compiled a 160-page review demonstrating that when hostile conditions are created in a petri dish — very low nutrients, hard agar surface — complex branching patterns emerge. Colony pattern is a readout of the boundary conditions: substrate stiffness, nutrient diffusion field, container geometry (Ben-Jacob et al., 2000).

The agar is not neutral. It carries the shape of its container into the resource gradient it presents to the organism. A circular dish creates a radially symmetric gradient with no preferential direction. A square dish creates four edge gradients and four corner convergence points. The organism does not know the difference. The agar does.

## 3. The Organism Navigates by Border

This is not speculation. The mechanism is one of the most studied phenomena in microbiology.

Julius Adler established the modern study of bacterial chemotaxis in 1966, showing that *E. coli* sense attractants and repellents via specific chemoreceptors and move along chemical gradients (Adler, 1966). Berg and Purcell then derived the physics: because a bacterium is too small to measure a spatial concentration difference across its body, it performs temporal comparisons — sampling concentrations over time as it moves (Berg & Purcell, 1977). The bacterium extends its runs (straight-line swimming) when moving up a favorable gradient and tumbles (random reorientation) when the gradient flattens or reverses. The gradient IS the information. The boundary between concentrations is what the cell reads.

This is the same mechanism at every scale:

**Mycorrhizal fungi navigate by gradient.** Sbrana and Giovannetti showed that hyphae of *Glomus mosseae* reorient their growth direction toward host roots, detecting host-derived chemical signals from nearly a millimeter away — and reorientation occurs only in the presence of a host plant (Sbrana & Giovannetti, 2005). Akiyama, Matsuzaki, and Hayashi identified the signal molecules — strigolactones, plant sesquiterpenes — that trigger extensive hyphal branching in arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (Akiyama et al., 2005). Simard and colleagues demonstrated that mycorrhizal networks transfer carbon bidirectionally between trees via shared fungal connections, using isotopic tracers — the paper that launched the "wood wide web" concept (Simard et al., 1997). The fungal network is not a passive pipe. It navigates resources along gradients between connected organisms. The border between two soil conditions — two nutrient levels, two host root systems, two moisture zones — is where the hyphae go.

**Physarum polycephalum "solves" mazes by the same mechanism.** Nakagaki, Yamada, and Toth placed slime mold in a maze with food at two endpoints. The organism filled the maze, then retracted from dead ends and converged on the shortest path (Nakagaki et al., 2000). This was published in *Nature* and reported worldwide as an amoeba displaying intelligence. In a follow-up, slime mold grown with food sources placed at locations corresponding to Tokyo-area cities produced a transport network comparable in efficiency to the actual Tokyo rail system (Tero et al., 2010).

But the organism is not solving anything. A maze is a structure made entirely of boundaries. Every wall is an edge. Every turn is a gradient change. An organism that navigates by boundary, placed in a structure composed entirely of boundaries, arriving at a resource — that is not intelligence. That is a tautology. The experimental design guaranteed the result.

The actual test would be to remove the walls. Place the organism in open flat agar with the food at the same distance, no boundaries between them. Without a gradient to follow, the organism has no signal to navigate by — the physics of chemoreception require a differential, and a uniform medium presents none (Berg & Purcell, 1977). The food is present but the medium is flat. There is nothing to follow. The walls were the signal. The food was just where the walls ended.

## 4. The Instrument Is the Variable

The petri dish was invented by Julius Richard Petri in 1887 as a tool for isolating bacterial cultures. It was designed to be a controlled environment — a neutral stage on which the organism performs. For 139 years, the dish has been classified as apparatus.

But apparatus that shapes what it observes is not apparatus. It is a variable.

The glass creates the boundary. The agar carries the boundary into the medium as a resource gradient. The organism reads the gradient. The colony pattern is a readout of the container geometry filtered through the medium, not a readout of the organism's behavior in isolation.

This means that every colony morphology study conducted in a petri dish includes an uncontrolled variable: the shape of the dish. A circular dish and a square dish will produce different colonization patterns from the same organism under the same conditions — not because the biology changed, but because the map changed.

The literature already contains the evidence for each step in this chain:
- Agar is non-neutral (Fujikawa & Matsushita, 1989; Matsushita & Fujikawa, 1990; Ben-Jacob et al., 2000)
- Organisms navigate by gradient (Adler, 1966; Berg & Purcell, 1977; Berg, 2004)
- Colony pattern depends on medium boundary conditions (Budrene & Berg, 1991; 1995)
- Fungal networks navigate along resource borders (Simard et al., 1997; Sbrana & Giovannetti, 2005)

No one connected the chain. The glass was invisible because it was filed under "apparatus." You don't question the ruler. But the ruler is drawing the lines.

## 5. The Border Has Always Been the Function

The petri dish observation is a specific case of a general principle that the biological sciences have been documenting for over a century without stating it: **the boundary is not where function stops — it is where function lives.**

**Endosymbiosis.** Lynn Margulis proposed in 1967 that mitochondria, the structures every biology student learns to call "the powerhouse of the cell," were once free-living prokaryotes (Sagan [Margulis], 1967). This was expanded in her 1981 book *Symbiosis in Cell Evolution* and later confirmed by genetic analysis of organellar DNA. The standard teaching is that the eukaryotic cell "acquired" a mitochondrion — as though it swallowed a generator and put it to work.

But what actually happened is that a prokaryote — an organism that was already performing membrane-level energy exchange with its environment, as every prokaryote does — took up residence at the boundary of a larger cell. The membrane that was already doing the work continued doing the work. The mitochondrion did not gain a new function upon engulfment. It kept the same function it had always performed: energy exchange at a surface. The eukaryotic cell did not acquire a powerhouse. It acquired a border that stayed alive.

The word "mitochondria" comes from the Greek *mitos* (thread) and *chondros* (grain). The name describes a connection — a thread between two sides. The observation that named it was correct: this is a relationship across a surface. The institution replaced the relationship with a machine metaphor — input fuel, output ATP — and the name's own meaning was buried under the description. The thread part, the part that says *this connects two sides*, was dropped because it did not fit the generator model. The name was right. The textbook was wrong.

Meanwhile, free-living prokaryotes outside the cell perform the same membrane exchange with their environment. The same chemistry, the same surfaces, the same input-output function. But it is described with entirely different language: "environmental metabolism" instead of "cellular respiration." The function did not change. The address changed. And the name changed with it.

**Crystal energy.** Jacques and Pierre Curie demonstrated in 1880 that mechanical pressure on certain crystals generates surface electric charge (Curie & Curie, 1880). This is piezoelectricity — and it is not energy stored in bulk volume. It is a property of the crystal's structural asymmetry at its boundaries. Under stress, positive and negative charge centers shift within the unit cell, producing polarization at the surfaces. The energy is in the lattice relationships — in the boundaries between planes, in the faces of the crystal — not inside the crystal the way water is inside a tank.

Read and Shockley showed that grain boundaries in crystals carry excess free energy per unit area — energy localized at the structural boundary, not distributed through the bulk. Material near grain boundaries is preferentially etched away, directly demonstrating that stored energy concentrates at structural interfaces (Read & Shockley, 1950).

A crystal is not a container holding energy. A crystal is what boundaries look like when they become solid.

## 6. Every Natural Boundary Is an Exchange Surface

Consider the boundaries that exist in nature:

- A cell membrane: selective transport of molecules in and out. The boundary is the function.
- Skin: exchange of heat, moisture, sensation between organism and environment. The boundary is the function.
- A shoreline: exchange of nutrients, sediment, organisms between water and land. The boundary is the function.
- A root surface: exchange of water, minerals, sugars, and signaling molecules between plant and soil. The boundary is the function.
- A mycorrhizal interface: exchange of carbon and nutrients between fungus and tree. The boundary is the function.
- A mitochondrial membrane: exchange of electrons and protons to produce ATP. The boundary is the function.
- A crystal face: exchange of mechanical stress and electrical charge. The boundary is the function.

Every natural boundary is where sharing occurs — a positive trade in both directions. Even parasitism is an exchange: the parasite gives the host immune pressure that drives adaptation. Every natural border transacts.

Now consider the petri dish wall. It trades nothing. It is a termination surface — the boundary where exchange stops. The organism hits the wall and the gradient ends. No transaction, no sharing, no exchange. This is a boundary that nature never makes.

A petri dish is not a model of a natural environment. It is the precise opposite. It is what an environment looks like when the boundary is dead.

## 7. What the Simulation Shows

The accompanying interactive simulation runs four petri dishes in parallel:

|  | **Circle Dish** | **Square Dish** |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed rule** (take what you can) | Chaotic dispersion from center, boom-bust collapse | Corner-organized colonization, structured collapse |
| **Generative rule** (share what you need) | Sustained variable equilibrium, center-weighted | Sustained variable equilibrium, edge-organized |

All four dishes receive the same solar energy input, the same resource regeneration rate, and the same initial population. The only differences are the container shape and the behavioral rule.

The closed-rule populations collapse in both shapes — but the square collapses with visible spatial organization (corner clustering, edge corridors), while the circle collapses as a uniform dispersal from center. The container shape organized the extraction pattern.

The generative-rule populations sustain in both shapes — but the square sustains with edge-weighted distribution, while the circle sustains with center-weighted distribution. The container shape organized the cooperation pattern.

The organism did not change. The rule did not change. The glass changed, and the colonization pattern changed with it.

## 8. The Connection

The road is a horizontal boundary between vehicle and ground — a sensor surface measuring soluble bodies in motion. The deed is a legal boundary between person and property — an instrument that either trades (stock) or terminates (lock). The petri dish is a physical boundary between organism and environment — a surface that either exchanges (natural membrane) or terminates (glass wall).

In each case, the boundary is doing the work. The road shapes how bodies move. The deed shapes how ownership distributes. The dish shapes how colonies form. The institution in each case looks at what happens inside the boundary and calls it the subject's behavior — traffic patterns, market dynamics, colony morphology — when the boundary drew the map.

The finding underneath: the border is not the edge of the system. The border IS the system. Every function lives at a surface. Every exchange happens at an interface. Every organism navigates by gradient, and every gradient is a boundary between two conditions. When you shape the boundary, you shape everything inside it — not by constraining it, but by drawing the map it follows.

The petri dish proved it in 1887 and filed it under "apparatus." The crystal proved it in 1880 and filed it under "material science." The mitochondria proved it two billion years ago and the textbook filed it under "powerhouse." The mycorrhizal network proves it in every forest on the planet and the literature files it under "ecology."

It is the same finding. It has always been the same finding. The border is the function.
